# Positioning register: what the antecedent papers actually contain

Prepared 2026-09-19 (round AA, doc 91 D5) from the project's local full texts.
Both outside reviews of 2026-09-19 had only abstracts for several of these
papers and asked that no comparison be promoted until the texts were checked.
This file records, in our own words, the narrow facts the manuscript's
literature section is allowed to rely on, with a locator into the local copy
(`L` = line of the local markdown file; the copies themselves are local
research-library material and are never redistributed). Nothing here is a
novelty claim.

Extraction form, per paper: what was read; the model object compared; the
narrow claim supported; what the comparison does **not** establish.

## Eaton and Engers (1992), "Sanctions", JPE 100(5)

*Read:* full text (`PaperRefer/Sanctions/EconomicSanctions-Eaton-1992.md`).

* Two parties, a target action and a sender sanction in `[0,1]`, alternating
  moves, each choice standing for two periods; Markov-perfect and limit
  equilibria; separate discount factors, each only required to lie in `[0,1)`
  (L61–69, L87–91, L65). **Complete information**, stated explicitly (L330).
* The only payoff-relevant state is the rival's standing choice (L103); the
  irreversible-action case is absorbing (L272). There is **no political,
  reputational or capacity stock** (model sections L59–321).
* "Toughness" combines patience **and** a party's own cost of sanctions
  (L49, L150; footnote at L767 contrasts this with Rubinstein). Outcomes are an
  interval of steady states pinned by the two toughness conditions (L163–178),
  shrinking as either discount factor tends to one (L182).
* In the once-and-for-all case a **more patient sender can lengthen the delay**
  before compliance, because it schedules sanctions less often (L51, L319).
  Sender patience is therefore already double-edged in their model, through
  timing rather than through a political state.
* No interest groups; the conclusion says group interests were ignored (L326).

*Supports:* "both parties discount, with possibly different discount factors;
outcomes turn on relative toughness, which is patience together with each
side's own cost of sanctions; there is no political state."
*Does not support:* "both parties are patient"; "patience always helps the
sender"; any claim that they have no state at all (the rival's last move is
one).

## Kaempfer and Lowenberg (1988), AER 78(4)

*Read:* full text
(`PaperRefer/Sanctions/TheoryOfInternationalEconomicSanctions-KaempferLowenberg-1988.md`).

* No game between governments: a static political-market-clearing model in
  which the sanction level rises until pro- and anti-sanction pressure balance
  (L84); the same apparatus is reused for the target's policy (L131–134). No
  periods, no discounting.
* Interest groups are central: summed marginal willingness to pay, shifted by
  free-riding (political ineffectiveness) parameters (L53–82). There is **no
  weighted government objective**; "political weights" is a loose description
  of this paper.
* Ineffectiveness parameters respond to the *current* sanction level
  (L152–160): a comparative-static shift, not a law of motion. An informal
  future-threat channel is mentioned (L172).

*Supports:* "sanctions redistribute political effectiveness among interest
groups in a static political market."
*Does not support:* "governments choose strategically" (they do not, there);
"entirely silent on expectations".

## Kaempfer, Lowenberg and Mertens (2004), Economics & Politics 16(1)

*Read:* **abstract only** (the local file is a bibliographic note; the full
text was never obtained).

* Adapts Wintrobe's dictatorship model; the dictator chooses power and the
  loyalty and repression that produce it; these respond to sanctions and to
  their effect on opposition groups' effectiveness; rents may be created.

*Supports:* a positive description of the mechanism, as in the abstract.
*Does not support:* **any** statement about timing, dynamics or the absence
of continuation values. The manuscript must not make an exclusion claim about
this paper.

## Powell (2006), "War as a Commitment Problem", IO 60(1)

*Read:* full text
(`PaperRefer/PoliticalEconomy/WarAsCommitmentProblem-Powell-2006.md`).

* Complete information by design (L23, L84). Two actors divide a flow of pies;
  each can use power to lock in a payoff, which destroys part of the flow;
  lock-in payoffs shift over time (L131–143).
* The inefficiency condition is that the per-period shift in the distribution
  of power exceeds the bargaining surplus (L149–159). It **cannot be written
  without** lock-in (minmax) payoffs and a surplus to divide; the mechanism is
  a limit on what a rising party can credibly promise to transfer (L147).
* In his applications patience makes fighting *more* likely (L165, L193).
* He warns against using "commitment problem" as a catch-all (L27, L121).

*Supports:* "shares with the commitment-problem literature the feature that
current actions shift future power in the absence of commitment."
*Does not support:* "creates a shifting-power commitment problem": our model
has no offers, transfers, surplus division or use-of-force option.

## Acemoglu and Wolitzky (2024), Handbook of the Economics of Conflict, ch. 2

*Read:* full text
(`PaperRefer/PoliticalEconomy/MistrustAndMisperception-AcemogluWolitzky-2024.md`).

* Scoped to imperfect information (L36); claims to unify "a large portion" of
  the literature (L65), not to be an exhaustive taxonomy. Commitment problems
  appear only in footnotes and are deferred to another chapter (L602, L608).
  The word "sanction" does not occur.
* Three models: the security dilemma (a coordination game with bad types,
  L90–120); its repeated version with public signals, private signals or
  one-period memory (L222–230); the deterrence model, where the first mover's
  hawkish action is dominant and the second mover wants to match (L336–346),
  and **both players prefer mutual restraint to mutual aggression** (L352).
* In the repeated security dilemma the players are assumed **infinitely
  impatient**, to rule out dynamic incentives (L222). Forward-looking
  "leaders" who internalise future benefits are named as a direction for
  future research (L329–330).
* How conflict ends: with public misperceptions, learning and eventual peace;
  with private misperceptions, a permanent trap; with one-period memory,
  deterministic restarts, i.e. cycles (L247–301).
* In dynamic deterrence with public signals, patience supports a folk-theorem
  outcome with little conflict (L431–433).
* Future work named in the conclusion: richer political-economy settings
  including interest groups, within-group heterogeneity, third-party mediators
  (L443–445).

* With a perfectly observed action (`pi = 0`) the deterrence game's unique
  subgame perfect equilibrium has both players restrained, **although the
  hawkish action is dominant for the first mover**: she anticipates that
  restraint is met with restraint (L348, "the basic logic of deterrence"). So
  anticipated-response restraint at the stage is *in* their benchmark; it is not
  what distinguishes our stage game (added 2026-09-20 on the reader's finding).

*Supports:* "an information-centred account of conflict dynamics in which the
object carrying incentives across dates is a belief; the repeated security
dilemma switches patience off (L222), the dynamic deterrence model uses it
(L431–433)."
*Does not support:* "every source of persistent conflict is informational"
as a claim about the literature; "patience always helps peace there".

## Acemoglu and Wolitzky (2014), "Cycles of Conflict", AER 104(4)

*Read:* full text. Overlapping generations of two groups, one short-lived
player per period, types, one-signal memory with one-sided misperception
(L41–60). Unique sequential equilibrium; a bad signal starts a spiral and
spirals end deterministically because the posterior drifts back to the prior
(L93–114).

*Supports:* "informational spirals end from inside through Bayesian
updating."

## Acemoglu and Robinson (2008), "Persistence of Power", AER 98(1)

*Read:* full text. The payoff-relevant Markov state is the **regime**
`s ∈ {D, N}` (L156). De facto power is produced each period from that period's
contributions plus a shock (L111–129); no power variable enters the state and
the words "stock" and "accumulate" do not occur. Invariance (L326): with equal
elite organisational technology across regimes, the distribution over economic
institutions does not depend on the regime.

*Supports:* "endogenous political influence responding to incentives."
*Does not support:* "de facto power is a stock"; any analogy between their
invariance result and our asymptotic irrelevance of initial conditions.

## The shadow-of-the-future and commitment literature (added 2026-09-20)

Nine papers the author supplied on 2026-09-20, read from the local copies in
`PaperRefer/ConflictTheory/` (one note per paper, each stating exactly which
pages were read). Locators in this section are **journal page numbers**, not
lines of a local file. Two facts hold across all nine and are what the
manuscript may rely on: **each uses one common discount factor or none**, so
none switches the two players' continuation incentives separately and re-solves
the equilibrium; and **none models a coercive instrument short of war that
moves a political state in the opponent's polity**. Neither fact is a
literature-wide priority claim. **Asymmetric horizons as a device are not
ours**: see the entries for McBride and Skaperdas (2009 working paper, read on
2026-09-21) and Hausken (2005, abstract only) at the end of this section, and
Eaton and Engers above. The manuscript's first page was narrowed accordingly
in round AH (the reader's A4, 2026-09-21).

### Skaperdas and Syropoulos (1996), JEBO 29:355–372

*Read:* full text. Two-period symmetric guns-versus-butter game, complete
information, subgame perfection. Parameters are restricted to `kL <= 1` so that
the one-shot game, and any repetition of it without time dependence, has the
no-arms equilibrium (pp. 357, 360–361); second-period resources are proportional
to first-period payoff (eq. 7, p. 361). Then a higher discount factor or growth
factor produces arming (regions B–D, pp. 364–366) and lowers relative efficiency
in regions B and D, not in C (p. 368). They do not deny cooperative equilibria
in longer versions (pp. 357–358); the extension to other distribution processes
is a conjecture (pp. 370–371).

*Supports:* "that valuing the future can turn static cooperation into conflict
when today's outcome feeds tomorrow's position is established (Skaperdas and
Syropoulos 1996)"; our design — static accommodation by construction, then the
intertemporal link — has this precedent.
*Does not support:* any decomposition by actor; an infinite horizon; "patience
always raises conflict" (region C).

### Garfinkel and Skaperdas (2000), JCR 44:793–807

*Read:* full text (scan, read as page images). Arming and fighting are distinct.
In one period both sides strictly prefer a negotiated division to destructive
war (pp. 796–800). With second-period resources proportional to first-period
payoff and the loser eliminated (eq. 9, pp. 800–802), no division is preferred
to war by both iff `phi(1+gamma) > 1 + gamma/(1+phi)` (eq. 15, p. 803); the
tolerated destruction rises with `gamma` (eq. 16, p. 804). No discounting; a
discount factor would act like `gamma` (fn. 13, p. 802). Longer horizons are
argued, not proved, to strengthen the result (p. 806).

*Supports:* "a static incentive to settle coexisting with a dynamic incentive to
fight, under complete information, is known."
*Does not support:* a non-terminal conflict, separate horizons, a political
state. Their channel is eliminating the rival.

### Powell (2004), APSR 98:231–241

*Read:* main text pp. 231–238; appendix proofs not worked through. Two-actor
stochastic game, common discount factor, full histories (p. 235). With `M_j(k)`
the **minmax payoff of the continuation game** from state `k`, Proposition 1
(p. 236): if on every efficient path some state has
`M_j(k) > B_k - [a_k^i + delta E_k M_i(n)]` for some player, there is **no
efficient Nash equilibrium**. The condition cannot hold in a repeated game
(p. 237). The opening sentence lists economic sanctions among the inefficiency
puzzles (p. 231); sanctions are not modelled. The conclusion asks for
microfoundations of the power shifts (pp. 237–238).

*Supports:* "all-equilibrium inefficiency results exist for complete-information
dynamic conflict, stated in continuation security values"; "that literature
asks for a microfounded source of shifting power."
*Does not support:* "our persistence is a Powell commitment problem" — we have
no offers, no divisible surplus, no efficiency benchmark between governments,
and `VSstar`, `VTstar` are equilibrium values, not minmax values.

### Leventoglu and Slantchev (2007), AJPS 51:755–771

*Read:* pp. 755–769; appendix skimmed. Complete information, finite resources,
no discounting, a temporary first-strike advantage; subgame perfection refined
to renegotiation-proofness (pp. 757–759). Fighting is unavoidable while
condition (P) holds and destruction eventually removes it (pp. 762–763); with
credible threats only, peace is possible at particular dates (condition (W),
p. 764; Example 1, pp. 765–766). The authors note that instruments that raise
costs by intensity or targeting, not duration, would change the conclusion
(pp. 761–762).

*Supports:* "an account of persistence must also say how the conflict can end";
history-dependent strategies and renegotiation are the standard against which a
conflict theorist will read a stationary equilibrium.
*Does not support:* any statement that sanctions end through accumulated cost.

### Acemoglu, Ticchi and Vindigni (2010), JEEA 8:664–676

*Read:* full text; the online proof appendix was not available. An elite facing
a rebellion chooses army size; a strong army ends the war but can later stage a
coup, and the elite cannot commit not to downsize it (pp. 665–668). Common
discount factor; unique Markov perfect equilibrium (Proposition 1, p. 673): a
weak army and a persistent war when the weak army's success probability exceeds
a threshold, more likely when the elite's rents are little affected by the war
(Corollary 1, p. 674). Persistence means long expected duration; only the elite
and the soldiers are strategic (fn. 4, p. 668).

*Supports:* "conflict can persist because ending it would shift domestic power
against the incumbent" — the nearest published analogue of our positive Sender
stake and Target loss-offset margin.
*Does not support:* a two-government game, a continuous political state, or a
role for separate horizons.

### Powell (2012), AJPS 56:620–637

*Read:* main text pp. 620–633; Lemma 1, Proposition 1 and Corollaries 1–2
checked on the page images. Infinite-horizon stochastic game, common discount
factor, Markov perfect equilibrium; the government offers, the rival accepts or
fights, and fighting forestalls an exogenous adverse shift (pp. 621–623; fn. 6).
Proposition 1 (p. 625): `R` fights in every MPE when `F_R - beta F'_R > 1`.
**Corollary 1 (p. 625): with a sufficiently high discount factor `R` fights in
every round.** The model fits best when concessions can be reversed at no direct
cost (p. 627, fn. 20).

*Supports:* "patience produces persistent fighting in a complete-information
dynamic game"; "reversible concessions are the hard case for commitment", which
is the case of sanctions relief.
*Does not support:* identifying our mechanism with his — there the weaker side
fights to stop the state moving and the binding constraint is on transfers.

### Powell (2013), QJE 128:807–859

*Read:* pp. 807–828 (introduction, model, Propositions 1–3) and the conclusion
pp. 841–842; Sections IV–VII and the proofs not read. The state is the
distribution of power, a point of the unit square, and the government's proposal
names the next state; this is called a costless reduced form (pp. 815–816).
Pure-strategy MPE (p. 818). The conclusion notes that only the government moves
the state and asks for a more symmetric, microfounded treatment (pp. 841–842).

*Supports:* "a continuous, endogenously moved state in a complete-information
conflict game is prior art."
*Does not support:* "our model supplies the symmetric treatment he asks for" —
in ours, too, one instrument moves the state.

### Krainin and Wiseman (2016), JOP 78:1139–1152

*Read:* pp. 1139–1146; the extensions, conclusion and online proofs not read.
N countries on a network, complete information, common discount factor,
stochastic finite-state strength, subgame perfect equilibrium with public
histories (pp. 1141–1142). **Theorem 1 (p. 1143): above some discount factor war
occurs in every SPE**, from every initial condition; Theorem 2 (p. 1144): below
some discount factor a peaceful SPE exists. They tie the discount factor to the
executive's expected tenure and regime type (pp. 1141, 1144–1145).

*Supports:* "that sufficient patience can make conflict unavoidable is an
all-equilibrium result in this literature"; an institutional reading of
discount factors.
*Does not support:* anything by actor — one discount factor throughout.

### Ruggiero (2026), AJPS 70(3), DOI 10.1111/ajps.70006

*Read:* abstract, introduction, model, comments on the model, conclusion, from
the publisher EPUB (no page numbers); propositions not worked through. A
pre-bargaining all-pay contest for agenda-setting power in an infinite-horizon
crisis-bargaining game; MPE; the persistence of war outcomes and of peace deals
are **exogenous parameters**, and their endogeneity is named as future work
(section "Comments on the model").

*Supports:* at most "the durability of an agreement is a parameter in recent
bargaining models of war".
*Does not support:* anything about sanctions.

### McBride and Skaperdas (2009), working paper, 22 May 2009

*Read:* the whole working paper (45 PDF pages), by an assistant reader and
spot-checked against the text (introduction, the model pages and Appendix B
read directly); held in `PaperRefer/ConflictTheory/`. Locators are PDF pages of
that version. **The published version (JEBO 105 (2014): 75–89) is not held, and
whether it keeps Appendix B is unverified; cite the version read.**
Main model (pp. 10–14): two agents, indefinite horizon, one discount factor
entering both payoffs (p. 13), a Markov perfect equilibrium; conflict is the
outcome above a critical discount factor because conflict today removes the
loser and the future cost of enforcement (eq. 6, p. 14). Appendix A
endogenises arming (pp. 24–27). **Appendix B (pp. 27–29): a long-lived agent
with an indefinite horizon faces a new one-period challenger each period; only
the long-lived agent's discount factor is varied, and the set of parameters
for which it goes to court grows with it (inequality (19), p. 29).** The
challengers have no continuation incentive. Nothing on sanctions or on a
political stock moved by a graded coercive instrument.
*Supports:* "a long-lived party facing one-period challengers"; "only one
party's patience is varied"; "asymmetric horizons are not our innovation".
*Does not support:* that the two sides' patience is varied separately or that
several patient/impatient combinations are compared (they are not); any
all-equilibrium statement (the result is for a Markov perfect class, and p. 4
concedes coexistence with other equilibria); anything about the 2014 article.

*Read:* nothing beyond the publisher's abstract, by the outside reader

player facing a myopic one in the battle of the sexes. *May be said:* nothing

## Consequences for our own stage game (derived, verified by both reviews)

On the placable family, at a fixed sanction the Target strictly prefers less
compliance, `U_T(x,0,s) − U_T(x,a,s) = ℓ·a`; after the Sender's stage response
is substituted its unique one-period optimum is the partial compliance
`(u₀+rx)/k` that buys the zero sanction, with gain
`(u₀+rx)·(k(c−nx) − ℓq)/(kq) > 0`; and the Sender ranks the two diagonal
outcomes the other way, by `(u₀+rx)²/(2q)`. So the first mover's hawkish action
is dominant at a fixed response, as in the deterrence model, but the two
governments do **not** share a preference for the restrained outcome, unlike
that model (L352 above).

### Eaton and Engers (1992), Section IV and Figure 4 (held, re-read 2026-09-21)

* Section IV classifies outcomes by the target's cost of sanctions `F_T` and
  its discount factor: the boundaries are `F_T = 1`, `F_T = 1 − δ_T` and
  `F_T = 1/δ_T − δ_T`, with the sender's condition `F_S > δ_S`; in the linear
  example the interior steady state is `a* = (αF_T − F_S)/(αG_T + G_S)`
  (Figure 4 and the text around L321). "How much the threat of sanctions can
  extract depends on their costs to each party and the parties' relative
  patience" (L268). Outcomes are compared across Markov-perfect steady states.
* *Supports:* "toughness is patience together with own cost, for every
  Markov-perfect steady state"; "the closest antecedent classifies by the
  target's discount factor". *Does not support:* an exact threshold in one
  government's discount factor alone, or any state carried across dates.

### Eaton and Engers (1999), "Sanctions: Some Simple Analytics", AER P&P (held as `PaperRefer/Sanctions/2010-sanctions-some-simple-analytics.md`; read 2026-09-21)

* Introduction: success is more likely when the threatened measure costs the
  sender little relative to its gain and damages the target much relative to
  its cost of complying; the paper reports that a more patient sender is more
  likely to succeed and that the target's patience works against the sender
  (L19 and the introduction's summary of results).
* *Supports:* "the two sides' patience is signed in opposite directions in the
  closest antecedent". *Does not support:* a non-monotone or two-sided switch
  in either discount factor; a political state.

### Drezner (2003), "The Hidden Hand of Economic Coercion", IO (held, `PaperRefer/SanctionsTheory/Drezner2003.md`)

* The standing objection: under complete information the target concedes at
  the threat stage, so imposed sanctions are the cases where bargaining failed
  or information was incomplete; imposed sanctions select for the hard cases.
* *Supports:* stating the complete-information objection and answering it
  with what is proved (the political state is what makes imposition an
  equilibrium outcome at complete information). *Does not support:* any
  duration claim.

### Dixit (1989) and Krugman (1991) (held, `PaperRefer/MathTheory/Hysteresis-Dixit-1989.md`, `HistoryVersusExpectations-Krugman-1991.md`); Arthur (1989) (reader)

* Dixit: hysteresis as a band of inaction from sunk costs under uncertainty;
  the state is moved by optimising entrants. Krugman: history versus
  expectations, with the real-root case in which initial conditions alone
  select the long-run outcome. Arthur: the four-property taxonomy of
  increasing-returns processes (reader's report).
* *Supports:* "the slow state is moved by optimising agents in all three".
  *Does not support:* the manuscript's former contrast "moved by an
  optimising action" (removed in round AI, T4); the real difference is two
  strategic governments and an instrument that moves the state.

### Hassler, Rodríguez Mora, Storesletten and Zilibotti (2003), "The Survival of the Welfare State", AER (held)

* Proposition 2: a pro-welfare equilibrium with persistent redistribution;
  two basins inside one equilibrium built by splicing two rules, and in §4.2
  the rival equilibrium is excluded (L198, L304).
* *Supports:* "dual-basin pictures inside one equilibrium are known".
  *Does not support:* two strategic governments or a sanction instrument.

### Acemoglu, Egorov and Sonin (2012), "Dynamics and Stability of Constitutions, Coalitions, and Clubs", AER (held)

* Theorem 2: with discount factors sufficiently close to one **and small
  transaction costs from changing states** (both are assumptions of the
  theorem, L19), every equilibrium of the dynamic game corresponds to a
  stable-state mapping φ, and conversely. The central object maps each initial
  state to its stable state; the discount threshold is pinned by explicit
  inequalities and is not displayed in closed form.
* *Supports:* citing it for the initial-state-to-stable-state map beside the
  dual-basin result. *Does not support:* an exact closed-form threshold.
  **Correction to doc 98 §4.1 (E3) and doc 99:** the round's verifier and I
  wrote that "small transaction costs" is not an AES assumption; the held
  abstract states it in terms. The reader was right on that clause.

### Bressan and Jiang (2020), "Self-consistent feedback Stackelberg solutions", (held in full, `PaperRefer/StochasticGames/`)

* Existence of feedback Stackelberg solutions under a far-sighted leader or a
  narrow-sighted follower; horizon-organised.
* *Supports:* naming it as horizon-organised Stackelberg work. *Does not
  support:* the claim that re-solving four horizon combinations is new in
  itself; what is new is the political state and the classification.

### Bucarey, Jean-Marie, Ordóñez and Ramírez (2023), stationary strong Stackelberg equilibria (held as a note)

* Re-solves at three horizon corners in a stochastic-game setting.
* *Supports:* the same sentence as Bressan–Jiang. *Does not support:* a
  two-sided threshold or a state moved by the instrument.

### Attia and Grauvogel (2023), *JPR* 60(4): 709–719, the IST dataset (reader; not held — the text held under `SanctionsEmpirics/AttiaGrauvogel2023.md` is a handbook chapter, see above)
